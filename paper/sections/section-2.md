# 2. Related Work

## 2.1 Global Path Planning in Grid Environments

Global path planning aims to find a collision-free path between two points in a known static environment. Classical graph-search algorithms remain the dominant approach for grid-based planning. Dijkstra's algorithm guarantees the shortest path but explores uniformly in all directions, making it inefficient for large maps. The A* algorithm improves efficiency by incorporating a heuristic function $h(n)$ that guides the search toward the goal while preserving optimality when $h(n)$ is admissible. However, standard A* uses a fixed heuristic weight, which forces a trade-off: a weight of 1 guarantees optimality at the cost of expanded nodes, while a larger constant weight speeds up search at the cost of suboptimal paths. Several A* variants address this limitation. Weighted A* multiplies the heuristic by a constant factor $\varepsilon > 1$, producing $\varepsilon$-optimal solutions faster. Anytime Repairing A* and its descendants interleave weighted and unweighted searches to produce a sequence of improving solutions. However, these methods apply a uniform weight across the entire search, whereas the optimal weight intuitively depends on distance to the goal: aggressive weighting is beneficial far from the goal, while precision is needed near the goal. Our adaptive exponential weighted heuristic (Section 4.1.1) addresses this by decaying the weight smoothly with proximity to the goal.

Sampling-based planners such as Rapidly-exploring Random Trees (RRT) and Probabilistic Roadmaps (PRM) offer probabilistic completeness and are effective in high-dimensional configuration spaces, but their solution paths in grid environments are typically longer and less consistent than those of graph-search methods due to their reliance on random sampling.

Post-processing of grid paths is a well-established practice. Path pruning methods remove redundant colinear waypoints via collinearity checks or line-of-sight verification. Path smoothing methods, including cubic splines, B-splines, and clothoid curves, convert discrete waypoint sequences into continuous curves suitable for robot execution. However, pruning and smoothing are typically treated as independent post-hoc steps; their integration into the cost matrix computation—where simplified path lengths inform the combinatorial optimization—is a distinguishing feature of our framework.

## 2.2 Metaheuristics for the Traveling Salesman Problem

The Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) is one of the most intensively studied problems in combinatorial optimization. Exact algorithms based on branch-and-bound or dynamic programming are feasible only for small instances (typically $K \leq 20$) due to the factorial growth of the search space. For larger instances, metaheuristics are the methods of choice.

Genetic Algorithms (GA) encode tours as chromosomes and apply crossover (e.g., order crossover, OX) and mutation operators, selecting the fittest individuals via tournament or roulette selection. Simulated Annealing (SA) explores the solution space via neighborhood moves (typically 2-opt swaps) and accepts worsening moves with a probability that decays according to a cooling schedule, allowing escape from local optima.

Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) is a population-based metaheuristic inspired by the foraging behavior of ants. In the basic Ant System, ants construct tours probabilistically based on pheromone trails and heuristic visibility, and deposit pheromone on their tours to guide subsequent generations. The Ant Colony System extends this with a pseudo-random proportional transition rule that balances greedy exploitation with probabilistic exploration, improving convergence speed. The Max-Min Ant System further stabilizes the search by imposing explicit upper and lower bounds on pheromone values, preventing premature convergence on suboptimal solutions and preserving exploration diversity on rarely-used edges.

Local search hybridization has proven highly effective in ACO. Applying 2-opt, 3-opt, or Variable Neighborhood Descent (VND) to the best solutions of each generation significantly improves solution quality. However, VND evaluates $\mathcal{O}(K^2)$ candidate moves per neighborhood operator, which becomes a computational bottleneck for $K > 100$. Our candidate-list-accelerated VND (Section 4.3.4) reduces this to $\mathcal{O}(K \cdot k)$ by pruning unpromising evaluations using a precomputed $k$-nearest-neighbor list, making local search practical for larger problem instances.

Progressive local search scheduling has received less attention in the literature. Most ACO implementations apply local search to a fixed fraction of ants throughout the run. Our S-curve scheduling (Section 4.3.5) increases the local search fraction from zero to a maximum over the iteration budget, matching the natural shift from exploration-dominated early search to exploitation-dominated late refinement.

## 2.3 Multi-Point Traversal with Obstacle Constraints

The intersection of path planning and TSP optimization has been explored under several labels. The TSP with Obstacles (TSPO) considers a set of polygonal obstacles in a continuous plane and seeks the shortest tour visiting all target points without intersecting obstacles. Solutions typically compute visibility graphs and solve the TSP on the resulting graph. The Dubins TSP extends the problem to vehicles with minimum turning radius constraints. The TSP with Neighborhoods generalizes the target from a point to a region, requiring the tour to visit each region at least once. These formulations rely on continuous geometric representations (polygons, disks) and assume specific obstacle shapes, limiting their applicability to grid-based robot navigation where obstacles are represented as arbitrary occupancy patterns.

Two-layer architectures that combine a path planner with a TSP solver have been proposed. For example, Probabilistic Roadmaps have been used to construct a connectivity graph, with the TSP solved on shortest-path distances between sampled configurations. However, these approaches typically use off-the-shelf planners and solvers without optimizing either layer for the multi-point traversal context. The cost matrix is computed from raw grid paths, which overestimate the true path length due to discretization artifacts, and the TSP solver lacks the problem-specific enhancements (open TSP encoding, candidate-list acceleration) that our framework provides.

The open TSP variant—where the start and end points are distinct fixed locations—is particularly relevant to autonomous navigation missions, where a robot departs from a depot and ends at a destination. Much of the classical TSP literature, including most of the TSPLIB benchmark instances, addresses the closed TSP. The open TSP requires a different encoding and cost evaluation function, which we formalize in Section 3.2 and address algorithmically in Section 4.3.1.

To the best of our knowledge, no prior work combines (i) an improved grid-based global planner with adaptive heuristic weighting and safety-aware path post-processing, (ii) an open-TSP ACO solver with candidate-list-accelerated VND and S-curve progressive local search, and (iii) a collaborative cost-matrix interface that uses simplified-path costs for accurate combinatorial optimization, into a single unified framework for obstacle-aware multi-point traversal path planning.
